\subsection{Where encryption sits in the health-records security literature}
\label{sec:ehr-lit}

Surveys of security and privacy for electronic health data organise the field by component, data, devices, networks and cloud, and by requirement, confidentiality, integrity, availability, access control and audit~\cite{oh2021ehealthsurvey,keshta2021ehr}. Within that map, encryption at rest is treated as a settled requirement and research attention has gone elsewhere: to \emph{who may decrypt}, through attribute-based encryption of personal health records so that policy is enforced by the key rather than by the server~\cite{li2013abe}; to \emph{sharing and provenance}, through blockchain-anchored EHR systems~\cite{nguyen2019blockchainehr,shi2020blockchainehr}; and to \emph{application authorisation}, through the SMART on FHIR profile that standardises how third-party applications obtain scoped access to a record~\cite{mandel2016smartfhir}. These lines of work are complementary to this paper. They decide and enforce who is allowed to read a record; none of them asks whether the bytes the server returns for that record are the bytes that were written for it. That question, integrity of association at the storage layer, is the subject here.

\subsection{Hybrid and chaotic encryption for health records and medical images}
\label{sec:genre}

A separate and active literature has pursued security \emph{beyond} a standard cipher for health data, most often for medical images. Its recurring design places a keyed, reversible transform before, inside or instead of a block cipher: a chaotic map that permutes or diffuses bytes or pixels, a substitution box generated from a chaotic sequence or an algebraic structure, or a chaotic system used as a keystream. The resulting schemes are described as hybrid, improved, enhanced or lightweight, and the added stage is presented as additional security. Table~\ref{tab:genre} characterises twelve such schemes from their published abstracts, grouped by whether a standard cipher remains in the pipeline.

Three features of this literature motivate Section~\ref{sec:pretransform}, and they hold across both groups in the table. First, the evidence offered is statistical in every case: entropy, histogram uniformity, correlation, key sensitivity, or the NPCR and UACI differential measures computed on ciphertext. Where a standard cipher remains in the pipeline, such a statistic is a property of that cipher and would be observed with the added stage removed; where none remains, the statistic describes the construction itself, and no abstract offers a reduction to a standard assumption. This is not a new observation within the chaos community. {\'A}lvarez and Li set out in 2006 the requirements, on implementation, key management and security analysis, that chaos-based cryptosystems should meet and noted that many proposals did not~\cite{alvarez2006requirements}; later designers acknowledge that ``certain chaos-based cryptosystems have been proven to exhibit various security defects''~\cite{hua2019cosine}, and cryptanalyses that break published schemes with chosen-plaintext attacks continue to appear~\cite{feng2021cryptanalysis}. Recent reviews of chaos-based and medical-image encryption survey the design space without resolving the evaluation question~\cite{zhang2023chaosreview,ahmed2023mireview}. Second, where an abstract describes how the transform key reaches the receiver, it travels with the data or is derived from the plaintext; most abstracts do not say, and a scheme whose receiver cannot reconstruct the key cannot decrypt. Third, and most relevant here, none of the twelve binds a ciphertext to the record, patient or field it was written for. Each is a confidentiality mechanism for bytes and is evaluated as such.

The word \emph{hybrid} also names a different and sound design, which we distinguish to avoid conflating the two. Several health-data schemes combine an asymmetric algorithm for key establishment with a symmetric cipher for the data: AES with RSA behind wavelet-domain steganography~\cite{elhoseny2018iot}, a modified AES with RSA for a hospital cloud database~\cite{zhang2019paes}, elliptic-curve key exchange with AES-128 in EAX mode for smart-home health devices~\cite{popoola2024smarthome}, or with AES for cloud-stored mental-health records~\cite{selvi2025symeccipher}; Chowdhary et al.\ compare several such pairings~\cite{chowdhary2020hybrid}. This is the standard key-encapsulation composition and nothing in Section~\ref{sec:pretransform} applies to it. We note that these schemes are nonetheless evaluated with the same speed tables and, for images, the same statistics, and that although one of them uses an authenticated mode~\cite{popoola2024smarthome}, none describes binding a ciphertext to its storage context. Section~\ref{sec:pretransform} implements the most common pre-transform instance from its published description and measures exactly what it adds; Section~\ref{sec:design} then addresses the binding that all of these schemes leave to the database.
