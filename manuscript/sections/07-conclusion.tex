\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A cipher name and a key length specify the confidentiality of bytes. They say nothing about whose bytes they are, and an insider who can write to the store can exploit that gap without breaking anything. We have shown the attack against the two most common deployments of AES for clinical records, and presented CPDPM, which closes it by treating the context of a ciphertext, its tenant, patient, resource type and field path, as an input to encryption rather than as metadata around it. The context is encoded so that two contexts cannot collide, derives a key of its own from a versioned master key, and is bound again as associated data. A key commitment removes a class of attacks on password-derived keys, versioned keys make retention a cryptographic fact, an encrypted manifest binds the set of encrypted fields in a FHIR resource, and a streaming mode carries the same guarantees to large payloads. Security reduces to the standard AEAD notions.

The construction rejects every storage-layer manipulation the baselines accept, at a fixed cost of a few microseconds per operation over raw AES-GCM and well below the most common convenience library from ten records upward. We have also shown that the byte-level pre-transform layers proposed elsewhere for the same problem add nothing when their key travels, add no diffusion in any case, and for the most common instance have a keyspace of $2^{\KeyspaceLog}$ by exhaustive check, and that the engineering they absorb is better spent on binding.

One rule carries the design into deployment: the context must come from what the application knows to be true, never from what it reads back from storage. The implementation, tests, known-answer vectors and every experiment in this paper are released so that the claim can be checked.
